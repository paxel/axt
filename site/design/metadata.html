An app that gets a type it has never seen still has the schema. The attributes in it tell that app how to show each field, in its user's language, and which values are valid. They are worth writing for every type you send.

Hints and checks

An attribute is a member of the schema's JSON whose name begins with axt. (metadata, section 1). There are two kinds:

  • A hint says how to show a type, a field or a symbol: a label, a help text, an emoji, an order. A value cannot break a hint.
  • A check is a rule a value can break: axt.format, axt.pattern, axt.min_length, axt.max_length, axt.min and axt.max.

Every attribute is optional. The attributes of a field sit on the field, not on its type (section 3.1). They travel with the schema, in the offer and in the file header, and no attribute changes the schema's fingerprint (section 3.4). The table of section 2 lists every attribute with its limits.

Labels, help and emoji

  • axt.label is the name a receiver shows for the type, a nested type or a field, in English, such as Postal code. axt.help is one or two sentences on what the value is or how to enter it, such as Five digits. Both are one line of plain text; a label is at most 256 bytes, a help text at most 1,024 (section 4.1).
  • The label and the help text are written for the user. The doc is written for the developer and stays the definition of what the field means.
  • axt.label_translations and axt.help_translations give the same texts in other languages, keyed by language tag, such as de or pt-BR, never en. A translation needs its English text. A receiver picks the language from its user's list of languages and falls back to English (section 4.2).
  • axt.emoji is one emoji a receiver may show next to the label, such as 📞 for a phone number. It should be one emoji that Unicode recommends for general interchange, and is never shown in place of the label (section 4.3).
  • axt.symbols on an enum gives each symbol a label, help, emoji and their translations, since Avro gives symbols no attributes of their own (section 4.4).

Without a label, a receiver shows the field's name with underscores read as spaces (types, section 6). Every example type carries a label and an emoji on its record, its nested records and its fields (types, section 5.9).

Order and groups

  • axt.order on a field puts it before the fields without one, the lowest first. Fields without it keep the order of the schema. The order is for display only; the encoding keeps the order of the schema, and its range lets a type put a new field before those it already has (section 5.1).
  • axt.group on a field names a group, such as contact; fields with the same key are shown together. axt.groups on the record gives each group its label, emoji and translations once (section 5.2).

Inputs

For a receiver that lets its user enter or change a value (section 5.3, 5.4):

AttributeWhat it says
axt.placeholderThe text an empty input shows, such as +49301234567. It is not translated, so it should be a form any reader understands
axt.exampleAn example value as text. On a field of strings it meets the field's checks
axt.multilineThe value may hold several lines
axt.preferred_lengthHow many code points an input should have room for, such as 5 for a postal code. It is not a limit
axt.sensitiveThe value is a secret or personal data that others near the screen should not see, such as an account number
axt.advancedMost users need not see or fill in the field

axt.sensitive only changes how the value is shown. It does not protect the value beyond what every message has: every message is signed, and sealed through a relay (section 8).

Checks

  • axt.format names the form of a text: plain, markdown, email, phone (E.164, such as +49301234567), url, uri, colour, iban, bic, country, language, currency, media-type or ucum. A receiver shows and offers inputs as the format suggests. Formats are checked by their syntax only, never against a registry (section 6.3).
  • axt.pattern is a regular expression in the I-Regexp form of RFC 9485 that the whole value matches, such as [0-9]{5}. I-Regexp has no anchors and no \d (section 6.4).
  • axt.min_length and axt.max_length bound a text in code points, or bytes in bytes. axt.min and axt.max bound a number, in the field's unit (section 6.2).

Three rules decide what a check covers (section 6.1):

  • null keeps every check.
  • The empty string keeps every format and every pattern. A field that needs a value says so with axt.min_length 1.
  • On an array, a check applies to each item, not to the array. The number of items is not checked.

Dates, times and ids have no formats: they are the time types and the logical type uuid.

What a check binds

A check binds your app as a sender; it only lets the receiver refuse (section 7.1):

  • Your app never sends a record with a value that breaks a check of the schema it writes the record with.
  • A receiver may check the values against your schema and may reject a record that breaks one with VALUE_INVALID, naming the field, such as address.postal_code, so that your user can correct it.
  • A receiver that takes such a record anyway keeps and shows the value as it came. It never changes the value to meet the check, such as by removing the spaces of a phone number.

That is why adding or narrowing a check is not a compatible change, while widening or removing one is, and why labels and translations can change in any version (types, section 3.3; Evolve a type).

Attributes of your own

Names beginning with axt. are reserved for the specification, and a schema uses no other axt. name and no format the specification does not define (metadata, section 3.3). Attributes of your own begin with your namespace, such as org.example.catalog.colour (types, section 6). A receiver ignores attributes it does not know, and ignores an attribute that breaks its rules, but never declines a type for one.

An example

The illustration com.example.Vet of metadata, section 9, carries nearly every attribute. Three of its fields: 

{"name":"phone","type":"string","default":"","doc":"…","axt.label":"Phone","axt.label_translations":{"de":"Telefon"},"axt.emoji":"📞","axt.format":"phone","axt.placeholder":"+49301234567","axt.example":"+49301234567","axt.group":"contact","axt.order":3},
{"name":"visit_minutes","type":"int","default":15,"doc":"…","axt.label":"Length of a visit","axt.label_translations":{"de":"Dauer eines Besuchs"},"axt.emoji":"⏱️","axt.unit":"min","axt.min":5,"axt.max":240},
{"name":"iban","type":"string","default":"","doc":"…","axt.label":"IBAN","axt.emoji":"🏦","axt.format":"iban","axt.sensitive":true,"axt.advanced":true,"axt.group":"billing","axt.example":"DE89370400440532013000"}

A receiver shows the type as 🏥 Vet practice to a user who reads English and as 🏥 Tierarztpraxis to one who reads de-AT. The phone number is stored as +49301234567; a receiver should show it grouped, such as +49 30 1234567, but never stores the form it shows. A receiver that checks may reject a record whose number holds spaces, naming the field phone (section 6.3).

Note

A label is the sender's word. A receiver that acts on a field because of its label, rather than because it knows the type, acts on what the sender says (metadata, section 8).
